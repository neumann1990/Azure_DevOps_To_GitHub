**In one line:** Documentary narration carries the section openers and transitions; the technical content is played straight.

## The decision

> **Kevin (4 Sep 2026):** Section openers only. The nature-documentary voice does the framing and the transitions; inside a section I teach normally. Sustaining full narration across 75 minutes is a bigger risk than the bit is worth, and a voice that sags in the back half is worse than never starting one.

**Decided.** This resolves the open question that was gating the outline. Two registers, clearly separated:

| Register | Where it appears | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| **Documentary** | Section opener cards, transitions between sections, the closer | Calm, declarative, faintly awed. Never winks at the joke. |
| **Straight** | Everything inside a section — mechanics, mappings, war stories, recorded walkthroughs | Normal conference-talk delivery. Dry humour is fine; narration is not. |

## Why this shape

- **The bit stays fresh.** Roughly six openers plus a closer means seven doses across 90 minutes. That's enough to establish the conceit and land the callbacks without wearing it out.
- **It's cheap to produce.** Seven narrated cards and their imagery, not seventy slides that all have to sustain a voice.
- **It survives running late.** If a section overruns, cutting technical detail doesn't damage the framing. Under full narration, cutting anything damages the voice.
- **It de-risks the recorded walkthroughs.** Video segments can be captioned or narrated normally without a jarring register change.

## Rules that follow from it

1. **The voice never appears mid-section.** Once the opener card is gone, the narration is gone. Mixing registers inside a section is what makes the conceit feel half-committed.
2. **Every section gets an opener card.** No exceptions — a section without one reads as a mistake rather than a choice. Six sections, six cards.
3. **The transition line is spoken, not just shown.** The card is the visual; the delivery is what sells it.
4. **The closer returns to the voice.** Bookending is what makes the whole thing feel designed rather than decorative.

## Section opener cards

One per section, in documentary voice. Working set — assign to sections once the outline exists:

- **"Migration Season Has Begun"** — the opener
- **"Watch as the Scrum Master Approaches Carefully"**
- **"Rare Footage of Successful Deployment"**
- **"The Senior Engineer Defends an Ancient Pipeline"** — *this one consistently gets laughs because everyone recognizes themselves*

> Needs two or three more to cover six sections. Candidates should follow the same construction: a documentary-caption cadence describing ordinary engineering behaviour as though it were animal behaviour.

**Tagline for the title slide:** *"Observe the DevOps Engineer in their natural habitat."*

## Visual devices

Style reference: National Geographic / Planet Earth.

- **Nature photography** — carries the opener cards
- **Migration routes** — maps with arrows; doubles as an actual migration-sequencing diagram, so it earns its place twice
- **Species classifications** — taxonomy cards for org roles and for Azure DevOps subsystems

> **Kevin:** The species-classification device is the one that can bleed into the straight sections without breaking the rule, because it's a *visual* convention rather than a spoken one. A taxonomy card labelling each subsystem in the scorecard is theme-consistent without being narration.

## Narration source material

Real documentary voiceover, collected as tone reference and raw material to parody. The register to imitate: calm, declarative, faintly awed, never winking at the joke.

> This is the story of the last great wildernesses and the animals that live there.

> The Great Plains of the world may appear empty, but they support the greatest gatherings of wildlife found anywhere on Earth.

> The plains of our planet support the greatest gatherings of wildlife on Earth. At the heart of all that happens here is a single living thing. Grass. This miraculous plant covers a quarter of all the lands of the Earth.

> This is our planet's hothouse, the jungle, the tropical rainforest. Forests like these occupy only 3% of the land, yet they're home to over half of the world's species. But how do so many different kinds of plants and animals find the space here to live alongside one another?

> There are thought to be nearly a million different types of fungi in the tropics, the vast majority still unknown to science. But one thing's for certain. Without fungi, rainforests could not exist.

> Everywhere you look, on land or in the ocean, there are extraordinary examples of the lengths living things go to to stay alive.

> It's a case of first-come first-serve.

> For some, the city is a place of opportunity. For others, it's a place of danger. But for all, it is a world of our making.

> **Kevin:** The last one is the closer. "A world of our making" lands hard when the slide behind it is somebody's 2016 classic pipeline.
